The codec: the model works in DINO space, not pixels

MIRA's world model never touches a pixel. A video representation codec turns every frame into a small grid of meaningful numbers. The world model learns to predict what those numbers do next. Then the codec turns the predicted numbers back into video. This chapter runs the codec's frozen encoder (a real DINOv3 network) live in your browser, so you can see what the world model actually "sees".

Role in one sentence The codec decides what space the world model has to predict in. Its encoder (frozen DINOv3) supplies meaning, its bottleneck makes things small enough for real time, and its decoder only has to paint pixels back.

Demo 1 · Run the frozen encoder yourself

MIRA uses DINOv3-L/16, a self-supervised vision transformer that Meta trained on about 1.7B images. MIRA never trains it; it stays frozen. Below, the smaller sibling DINOv3-S/16 (21M params, same training recipe) runs in a Web Worker on four synchronised player views. Each 16×16-pixel patch becomes a 384-dimensional vector. To make those vectors visible, we project them onto their top 3 principal components and show them as RGB.

What to notice In the PCA view, the field, walls, sky, cars and HUD get distinct colours. DINO groups pixels by what they are, not by their exact colour. Click the ball in one view and the ball lights up in the other views too, even when it is tiny or seen from another angle.
Why the world model cares If "a car" is always a similar vector, then "the car moves left" is a simple, smooth change in latent space. The paper's explanation: "nearby states map to nearby latents, so a wrong prediction stays valid and is absorbed." Small errors stay "on the manifold" instead of turning into noise.

Demo 2 · Squeeze it through the bottleneck

DINOv3's grid is too big to predict 20 times a second. MIRA adds one linear layer, a patchifier, that merges 2 (rows) × 2 (columns) neighbouring patches and 2 consecutive frames, and shrinks 1024 channels to 32. The result is one latent "cell" per 32×32 pixels, at 10 Hz. The paper also tried a parameter-free version: pooling plus a fixed PCA projection. That ablation (Table 4) scored nearly as well as the learned layer: gFID 11.7 vs 10.7. The demo below builds exactly that PCA + pooling bottleneck live on the features you just computed.

The decoder: only a painter

The decoder is the only part of the codec that has to deal with pixels. It is a 0.45B-parameter space-time ViT:

  1. Upsample first: → tokens, so the attention runs at DINO's finer grid. The paper reports that this one step is "important for reconstruction quality" (Table 25).
  2. Attend: full attention within a frame, causal across time (it only sees past latents), so it can stream.
  3. Temporal unpatchify: each 10 Hz latent becomes 2 video frames, 50 ms apart → 20 fps.

It is trained with L1 + LPIPS + P-DINO. P-DINO re-encodes the output with DINOv3 and asks that the features match the original's. The two perceptual weights are rebalanced at every step so their gradients match the L1 gradient. There is no GAN loss and no KL/noise on the latent; the latent is deterministic.
