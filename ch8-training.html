How MIRA is trained, step by step, and what it costs

The earlier chapters explain what each part does. This one explains how each part learns: in what order, from what data, with which loss, and on how many GPUs. The key fact: training happens in separate stages, and each stage freezes everything it doesn't train. The decoder never sees the world model, and the world model never needs the decoder.

First: what "a training step" means at this scale

It's the same loop you wrote in makemore. Only the sizes change:

# makemore # MIRA (each stage) Xb, Yb = random batch clips = random batch of 4-s clips logits = model(Xb) pred = model(clips) # forward loss = F.cross_entropy(...) loss = (pred - target)²… # how wrong? loss.backward() loss.backward() # gradients p.data += -lr * p.grad optimizer.step() (AdamW) # update

Two things are new:

  • Frozen parts: some modules run in the forward pass but get no gradient update (requires_grad = False). Gradients stop there.
  • Many GPUs ("DDP", data-parallel): each GPU holds a full copy of the model and processes its own slice of the batch. After backward() the GPUs average their gradients, so every copy takes the identical update. 32 GPUs ≈ a 32× bigger batch in the same time.

Stage 1 · Training the codec (bottleneck + decoder)

Your mental model, checked "Take an input image, run it through DINO, run it through the compressor, run the decoder, and compare to the original. No world model needed." That is correct, with three refinements:
  1. The compressor (bottleneck) is trained too, jointly with the decoder. Only DINOv3 is frozen.
  2. It trains on video clips (40 frames), not single images. The bottleneck merges 2 consecutive frames into one latent, and the decoder is causal in time, so it needs sequences.
  3. "Visual fidelity" is three losses: L1 (pixel difference), LPIPS (looks the same to a perception network), and P-DINO (re-encode the output with DINO and compare features to the original's). The weights are auto-balanced every step. There is no GAN.
It trains on single-player clips: every player view is encoded and decoded on its own.

Watch the three errors. On training frames the error keeps falling. On unseen stadium frames it falls too, though less: the decoder generalises within its world. On the desert arena it stays clearly worse, and the colours come out wrong: the decoder paints with the palette it learned. The latent says what is where, and the decoder supplies how it looks. The images are blurry because the demo only uses an MSE loss. MIRA's LPIPS and P-DINO losses are exactly what fixes that blur.

Stages 2–3 · Training the world model

The codec is now frozen. The world model only ever sees latents: it reads latents and is graded on latents. The decoder isn't used at all during world-model training. It is only switched on to look at results and to compute evaluation metrics. One training step:

  1. Sample clips. A 4-second chunk: 80 video frames per player plus the 9-key state per frame.
  2. Encode with the frozen codec encoder. 80 frames → 40 latent frames per player. Stack the 4 players: . This is the clean target, z₁.
  3. Corrupt it (diffusion forcing). Draw a random noise level τ independently for every one of the 40 frames and mix in Gaussian noise z₀: zτ = τ·z₁ + (1−τ)·z₀.
  4. Prepare the keys. Embed each player's keys. With some probability, swap a player's keys for the "absent" token (action dropout).
  5. Forward. The transformer (causal in time) predicts a velocity for every token of every frame.
  6. Loss. Mean squared error between predicted velocity and the true direction (z₁ − z₀), averaged over all 40 frames. Each frame is a target and also context for later frames. On a fraction of steps, add the self-distillation loss (one big step = two half steps, Ch. 02).
  7. Backward + AdamW update (learning rate 1·10⁻⁴), only for the world model's weights. Keep a slow-moving average of the weights (EMA) for the final model.

Stage 2 does all of this with one player's view (a 9-row grid, one key stream) for 30k steps. Stage 3 continues from those exact weights with all four views and key streams for 100k steps. This warm start is needed: training multiplayer from scratch collapsed at the 1B budget (Ch. 05).

The big picture The final model is surprisingly cheap: roughly one to two days on one rack of 32 top-end GPUs for the world model, and less for the codec. At typical cloud prices (a few dollars per B200-hour) that's thousands to low tens of thousands of dollars. The hidden cost is everything around it: 26 days of data collection and the dozens of ablation and scaling runs behind the paper's tables, each roughly 50–200 GPU-hours. Research costs many times more than the final run.
How I estimated (click)

Rule of thumb: training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens processed (2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward). Frozen parts cost only the forward 2×.

World model, multiplayer: one 4-s clip = 40 latent frames × 576 tokens = 23,040 tokens. With an assumed 32 clips per step: 6 × 5·10⁹ × 23,040 × 32 × 100k ≈ 2.2·10²¹ FLOPs. Single-player stage (144 tokens per frame, 30k steps): ≈ 0.2·10²¹. A B200 does ~2.2·10¹⁵ dense BF16 FLOP/s. At a realistic 30–40% utilisation that is ~0.7–0.9·10¹⁵/s → ≈ 750–1,000 GPU-hours. Range widened for attention overhead and the distillation passes.

Codec: decoder 0.45B params × 32 clips × 20 latent frames × 576 tokens (6×) + frozen DINOv3-L forward on 1,280 frames (2×) + P-DINO network ≈ 1.5·10¹⁵ FLOPs per step × 400k steps ≈ 6·10²⁰ → ~200 GPU-hours at 40% utilisation. Video decoding and data loading probably make it slower in practice.

VM-hours: 120 VMs × 26 days × 24 h ≈ 75k (minus downtime).
