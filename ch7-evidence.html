How we know it works, and where it breaks

A world model's future is unknowable, so you can't just compare frames to ground truth. MIRA is judged on four axes: does it look real, does it obey the keys, does it get the physics right, and do humans agree. Then come the failures, and each one traces back to a specific component you met in earlier chapters.

The evaluation toolbox

Looks real?

gFID / gFVD / gFDD: distances between the distributions of generated and real clips (Inception, I3D and DINOv3 features). Tracked over the rollout horizon to expose drift.

Obeys the keys?

ARR, the Action Recoverability Ratio: how detectable each commanded key is in the generated video, divided by how detectable it is in a plain codec reconstruction. 1.0 = as faithful as possible.

Right physics?

Game-state probe: an MLP that reads ball and car positions, rotations and velocities out of the world model's activations. Trained on real latents, tested on the model's own rollouts.

Humans agree?

Bayesian Elo from pairwise human preferences, both for visual quality and for action adherence. ARR vs. human Elo: Pearson r = 0.84, Spearman ρ = 0.93.

ARR in miniature: run it yourself

The paper's recipe, reproduced on Rocket Pong:

  1. Train an action probe, an inverse-dynamics model that sees two consecutive states and guesses which key P1 pressed. Here it's trained on engine data.
  2. Roll the world model out from real starts using the real key sequence.
  3. Score the probe's detections with average precision on the generated transitions (APgen) and on the real ones (APreal).
  4. ARR = APgen / APreal, averaged over keys.

In MIRA the probe is a frozen DINOv3-B with a small attention head watching 8-frame video windows (0.84 mAP on real video), and the denominator is the codec reconstruction rather than the raw data, which cancels the codec's own losses.

What the paper found with ARR Controllability keeps improving long after image quality has plateaued: the model first learns to paint realistic frames, then spends most of training learning what the keys do. Rare keys (air-roll, reverse) score lowest, and ARR rises with how often a key appears in the data (r = 0.82). More unique data barely moves gFID past ~50 hours, but keeps raising ARR. Pixel-space models: ARR 0.49–0.61. MIRA's latent model: 0.91.

Failure modes, and which component causes them

Goal replays are made up

After a goal the game cuts to a replay, but by then the goal has scrolled out of the context. The model "simply makes something up". The multiplayer model sometimes diverges entirely during replays.

cause: 20-latent context window (Ch. 04/06)

The clock and score lose track

4:544:534:534:524:544:53

Five real seconds, sampled once per second (Fig. 19). The clock barely moves and even ticks back up. Score updates and clock milestones happen once per game, so they're rare in the data. Yet the errors stay self-consistent: when the model's own clock hits 0:30 it still shows the "30 seconds remaining" banner.

cause: short context + rare events in data

Out-of-distribution play "melts", then recovers

Park all four cars far from the ball and the views dissolve into noise. With the frozen-DINO codec the model snaps back on its own. The earlier distilled codecs stayed broken.

recovery: smooth DINO latent (Ch. 01) + diffusion forcing (Ch. 03)

Uncommanded boosts and jumps; the ball rolls by itself

In ~40 min of human play (≈48,000 frames): about 80 boosts and 30 jumps nobody pressed. They cluster at kickoff, where the bots always boost. A resting ball is rare in the data, so the learned prior keeps it moving toward a goal.

cause: data imbalance (one bot policy)
