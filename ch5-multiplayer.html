Four players, one world: attribution, consistency and autopilot

Most game world models take one action stream and treat everyone else as part of the scenery. MIRA conditions on all four players' keys and renders all four views jointly. That raises three questions. Does it learn whose key does what? Do the views agree with each other? And what happens to a player whose keys it doesn't get? You can poke at all three on a learned toy model below.

The three multiplayer ingredients

1 · Tiled views The four 9×16 latent grids are stacked into one 36×16 grid. Space attention spans all of them, so the ball, the clock and a demolition are rendered once, consistently, from four cameras (Fig. 5, 6, 17).
2 · Per-player actions Each player's keys go through a per-player embedding. They are concatenated in the same fixed order as the tiles and broadcast to every token through AdaLN. The model must learn which keys affect which pixels.
3 · Action dropout During training a player's embedding is randomly swapped for a learned absent token. To keep predicting, the model must infer that player's intent from the scene, so it learns the bots' policy as a side effect.

A single-player model is told only one player's keys. Everyone else's choices become hidden variables it has to guess. Guessing adds uncertainty, and over a rollout uncertainty turns into error. That's what the toy shows on the left, and it's what the paper finds at full scale: "multiplayer world models substantially outperform their single-player counterparts … the additional views and action streams reduce uncertainty about the future."

The single-player MIRA models also show the classic symptoms: a car that leaves the screen for longer than the context window is gone when the camera returns, and cars occasionally merge into the ball. The multiplayer model "largely avoids the first failure", and car–ball merging wasn't observed at all.

Training recipe Multiplayer from scratch collapsed at the 1B budget. Pre-training on single-player views and then continuing on 4-player tiles fixed it, because RoPE lets the same weights run on the bigger grid. The live model: 30k single-player steps, then 100k multiplayer steps.
