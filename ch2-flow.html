Flow matching: how the world model imagines the next frame

MIRA's transformer doesn't output the next latent frame directly. It outputs a velocity: a direction that slowly pushes random noise toward a plausible next frame. Here you'll train that exact objective live, on 2-D points instead of 18,432-number latents. Then you'll apply MIRA's trick (progressive self-distillation) that lets it jump from noise to frame in just 1–2 steps, which is what makes 20 fps possible.

Why generate instead of regress? The future is uncertain: the opponent might jump or might not. A plain regression to "the next frame" would average the possible futures into a blur. A generative model samples one sharp future. Flow matching is the generative objective MIRA uses.

1 · The training signal is a straight line

Take a real data point z₁ (in MIRA, the true next latent frame) and a pure noise sample z₀. Mix them:

zτ = τ·z₁ + (1−τ)·z₀   τ ∈ [0,1]
loss = ‖ vθ(zτ, τ, context, actions) − (z₁ − z₀) ‖²

The network sees the noisy mix and the noise level τ, and must say which way to move. On the straight line, the right answer is always the same arrow, z₁ − z₀. Drag τ to slide the noise points along their lines.

A single point zτ can lie on lines heading to many different targets. So the best the network can do is output the average direction. That averaging comes back to bite us in section 3.

3 · Teach it to take big steps (progressive self-distillation)

Running the 5B transformer 64 times per frame would be far too slow for 20 fps. MIRA's fix (following shortcut and consistency models) gives the network one more input, the step size Δ, and teaches it a self-consistency rule:

one step of size Δ ≈ two steps of size Δ/2
target = ½[ v(zs, s, Δ/2) + v(ẑu, u, Δ/2) ] (stop-grad)

Small steps are trustworthy because they come from plain flow matching. Through this rule, accuracy propagates up to Δ = ½ and then Δ = 1. At sampling time you tell the network "I'm taking one giant step", and it predicts the average velocity along the curved path instead of the instantaneous one.

In MIRA Few-step distillation runs on a random 10% of training updates (or with a fixed weight). The distilled model "matches many-step sampling quality in one or two function evaluations" and beats the baseline at every step count (Fig. 11).
